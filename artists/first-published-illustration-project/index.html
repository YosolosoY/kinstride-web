How to get your first published illustration project (with no portfolio yet)

If you are leaving art school with no portfolio, your first published credit usually comes from self-started or collaborative projects, not from paid commissions. Here is how to pick them, how to show them, and what to check before saying yes to any offer, ours included.

Start with work you decide

Career guides for illustrators with no commissions yet agree on one thing: begin with self-initiated work. A Penguin guide to becoming a book illustrator suggests self-started covers as a strong start for a newcomer, and quotes an illustrator who recommends including only pieces you are proud of and that reflect what you enjoy drawing.

A short, coherent series says more about you than many unrelated pieces. Choose a subject, a technique and a format, and keep them steady.

Show it where people look

Put the series online in a simple portfolio, on Behance or on Instagram. Central Illustration Agency adds that a website and social media are not enough on their own: becoming part of the illustration community (associations, talks, awards) matters too. And keep making things: being able to show that you create constantly helps you be found.

Some outlets feature new talent by email submission; Creative Bloq’s guide to getting illustrations published lists examples. Check that any list you use is still current.

A project that is not yours: what counts

A published collaboration, such as a game, a book or a magazine, adds something your own series cannot: someone else’s product with your name on it. It only helps if the credit is real and you can prove it.

What to check before accepting any offer

  1. Credit: your name and a link (Instagram, website or portfolio) on every piece, not only on a “thanks” page.
  2. Proof: a written statement of what you did, how many pieces and when.
  3. Licence: what the other side may do with the work, for how long, and whether it is exclusive.
  4. Portfolio rights: that you can show the work in your portfolio, social media and CV.
  5. Compensation: what, how much and when, in writing before you start. If the answer is “credit only”, that should be said plainly.
  6. AI: whether your art can be used to train AI or to imitate your style, and whether the other side uses AI on your files.
  7. Scope: number of pieces, format, deadlines and rounds of revision agreed up front.
  8. Reality of the project: is it published, or still in development? Many independent projects never reach release, so ask about its state.

After your first published piece

Ask for the written confirmation, add the piece to your portfolio with a link to where it is published, and tell people you are taking work: first commissions often come by word of mouth.

Frequently asked questions

Can I get a first published credit without clients?

Yes. Career guides for newcomers point first to self-initiated work, and a collaboration on someone else’s project adds a published credit if the credit is real and written down.

What should a credit look like?

Your name and a link to your Instagram, website or portfolio on each piece, plus a written statement of what you did, how many pieces and when.

Is a credit-only offer worth it?

It can be, if it is stated plainly, the licence is non-exclusive and you may show the work in your portfolio. Ask what you are getting, in writing, before you start.

How many illustrators does Kinstride accept?

We accept 2 new illustrators each month.

Does Kinstride pay?

Yes: US$50 once your first 10 pieces are accepted. Pieces accepted later carry your credit and are not paid additionally.
